Japanese shoe sizes: two mistakes even locals make

Updated April 14, 2026

The first time I tried on a pair of Asics in Tokyo, I thought my feet had shrunk overnight.
The shoe’s tongue whispered its secrets in centimeters, not numbers that made sense to me. That moment taught me Japanese shoe sizing isn’t about simple conversion—it’s about understanding how brands interpret the same system differently.

The worst part? Even people who’ve lived here for years fall into the same traps. They assume a US size 9 is always a Japanese size 27, or that all sneakers from the same brand will fit the same way. Neither is true.

Here are the two mistakes that trip up even seasoned shoppers—and how to avoid them when buying shoes in Japan.

Mistake one: Assuming all brands use the same centimeter measurement.

Take Asics and Mizuno, two brands that dominate the sneaker market in Japan. Both sell shoes labeled in centimeters, but their interpretations of “26.0 cm” don’t match.
I once ordered a Mizuno JP 26, only to realize it fit like a US 8.5—whereas an Asics JP 26 would have been too tight for my foot. The difference? Mizuno’s lasts run narrower, while Asics builds in slightly more room in the toe box.

Pro tip: Check the brand’s size chart for foot volume, not just length. Some brands like Adidas Japan offer “wide” versions of popular models, while others assume you’ll squeeze into their standard width. The difference between “standard” and “wide” in Japan isn’t just about width—it’s about how the shoe hugs your arch.

Mistake two: Ignoring the difference between EU sizes and Japanese sizes for imported shoes.

Many foreigners assume that shoes labeled “EU 42” in Japan will fit the same as a EU 42 bought in Europe. That’s rarely the case.
Japanese importers often shrink the last (the mold the shoe is built on) by 3-5 millimeters to appeal to local preferences for snugger fits. So a EU 42 that fits in Paris might feel tight in Tokyo.
I learned this the hard way when I bought a pair of Ecco dress shoes online. The EU 42 fit perfectly in Denmark, but in Japan it pinched my toes after an hour of walking. A quick check with the importer’s size chart showed their “EU 42” is actually cut for a foot 24.5 cm long—not the standard 25.0 cm.

Where to double-check before you buy:

Look for the brand’s official Japan size chart, not the global one. Most major brands have a separate JP chart on their Japanese website. If you can’t find it, email customer service and ask: “Is this shoe built on a Japanese last or an imported last?” That single question saved me from two wrong purchases last year.

Another insider detail: Seasonal adjustments. Some brands widen their lasts in winter to accommodate thicker socks. So a summer shoe might feel tight by December. Check the product description for terms like “冬用” (for winter) or “厚手靴下対応” (thick sock compatible).

For foreigners with wide feet, this system can feel especially frustrating. But there’s a workaround: department stores like Isetan and Mitsukoshi often carry imported wide sizes that aren’t available online. A quick trip to the physical store means trying on shoes before you commit—something rare in online shopping.

The shoes you choose shape the walks you take in Japan. A wrong size isn’t just uncomfortable—it turns every stroll into a reminder of the fit you couldn’t quite find.
